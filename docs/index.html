Omar

A keyboard for Omarchy. The commands you use to move around the desktop have keys of their own, labeled with what they do, around a standard US layout.

Scroll, and the board turns to show each part as you read about it. Drag it to turn it yourself.

Omarchy’s shortcuts follow a pattern

Omarchy is an Arch Linux setup built on Hyprland, a tiling window manager: windows are laid side by side rather than stacked on top of each other, and each workspace is a separate screen of them. It is meant to be driven from the keyboard, with Super as the desktop modifier. Super and a digit shows that workspace. Super, Shift and the same digit carries the focused window there. Add Alt and the window goes on its own while you stay put. Super and an arrow moves focus in that direction, and with Shift the two windows trade places.

The pattern is easy to learn and awkward to type. Sending a window away and taking it with you differ by one held key, Alt, and every desktop action is a chord on keys whose legends name something else.

Omar takes the actions people repeat dozens of times an hour and gives each one a key that names its result. The desk and window keys send the corresponding Omarchy chords. You can also type those chords directly. Omar calls a workspace a desk.

Three edges around a normal keyboard

The center of Omar is the board you already type on: US QWERTY, a full number row, both Shift keys and a full spacebar. The desktop controls sit around it, and each edge has one job.

Each group has its own cap color, for finding it by eye, and its own place on the board, for finding it by touch. A raised bar on Menu, Focus and Desk 5 marks the end of each group for a finger working by touch, the same way the homing bars on F and J mark the home row.

Five desks on the top row

Omarchy gives every monitor ten workspaces. Omar labels the first five and puts Desk 1 to Desk 5 so each one sits directly above the digit it stands for. Super with a digit still works, workspaces six to ten are still there, and Last Desk at the end of the group returns you to wherever you were before.

Moving a window takes two taps. Tap Send & Stay, then a desk, and the window goes there while you keep working where you are. Tap Move & Go, then a desk, and you go with it. The keys name the result because, with chords alone, the only difference between the two is whether Alt was held.

While a move is waiting, the five desk keys light up. It waits three seconds. Esc or a second tap on the same key cancels it. An arrow or any typing key cancels it and then does its usual job, so typing is never lost to a forgotten move. Holding a modifier, turning a dial or rolling the roller leaves it alone, so you can hold Shift or lower the volume in the middle of a move.

Windows under the right hand

Next Window sits beside the right Super key, where the right thumb can reach it. One tap moves to the next window on this desk. Shift goes the other way, and Fn goes back to the window you used last.

Focus and Swap sit in the bottom-right corner, beside the arrows, and work only while held. Hold Focus and press an arrow to move to the neighboring window, or hold Swap to trade places with it. Because they act only while held, brushing one by accident cannot turn the next arrow into a window command, and letting go hands the arrows straight back to the text cursor.

Above them, Flip Split turns a side-by-side pair into a stacked one, Float lifts a window out of the tiling, and Fullscreen gives it the whole display. Pressing the right dial changes the layout of the entire desk.

What made room

The edges needed space, and it came from three places: the function row, the six-key navigation block and the right-hand Ctrl.

Omar has no F1 to F12. The top row holds desks and utilities instead, and nothing on the Fn layer brings function keys back. Applications that depend on them need those shortcuts rebound. This is the largest compromise in the design. It suits Omarchy, where Super shortcuts do the work that function keys do elsewhere.

The navigation block became a scroll roller: a ribbed barrel 42 mm wide above the arrows, broad enough to catch with whichever finger is nearest, with thirty soft detents to a turn. Pull it toward you to read further down a page. Delete moved to the top row, directly above Backspace, so the two directions of deleting share a column.

The right-hand Ctrl became Alt. It sits beside the left arrow, so the right hand can hold it while using the arrows, and it sends a true Right Alt, which Omarchy uses for push-to-talk dictation and which some keyboard layouts need for accented characters.

Caps Lock still toggles when tapped. Held, it becomes Fn, and Fn does only what is printed on the keys in plum: Home, End, PgUp and PgDn on the arrows, Insert on Delete, Last Win on Next Window, split resizing on the right dial and paging on the roller.

Dials and utility keys

The two corner dials are knurled aluminum knobs. The left one sets the volume and mutes the speakers when pressed. The right one sets screen brightness and, pressed, cycles the desk’s layout. Mic Mute has its own key, so silencing your microphone never touches what you hear.

Lock ends the top row, next to the brightness dial, and is the one key there that interrupts your work. Its cap is about two millimeters lower than its neighbors and its switch takes 80 grams to press, so a finger sweeping along the row or reaching for the dial is unlikely to press it by accident.

Screenshot and Grab Text sit together. One captures an image of the screen; the other reads the text in a region and puts it on the clipboard as words you can paste.

It uses Omarchy’s own shortcuts

Desk travel, window transfers, focus, launchers and clipboard controls send Omarchy 4.5.0 shortcuts. These actions work with the stock bindings.

Optional host bindings open the physical key guide, recall the last window on a desk and provide the pilot’s microphone toggle. A separate setting limits keyboard travel to the five labeled desks. A small helper program lets the desk keys show which desk is active and lets Mic Mute show whether the microphone is live. Without it, the keys light only for what the keyboard itself knows.

The physical board

Case
422.8 × 137.1 mm, a 6° typing angle, about 20 mm high at the front edge
Controls
90 keys, two push-button dials and a scroll roller
Switches
MX-compatible in hot-swap sockets, so switch weights can be changed after testing
Keycaps
Cherry profile PBT with dye-sublimated legends
Electronics
RP2040 controller running QMK, USB-C on the back edge
Lights
Fifteen keys lit from below, for moves in progress, held window keys, the active desk, Caps Lock and the microphone

No physical board has been made yet. Omar exists as a full dimensional specification, a browser prototype, pilot firmware and host scripts with passing tests, and the model on this page, which is built from the same switch coordinates as the plate drawing. The next steps are a full-size paper fit test on a real desk, then a hand-wired pilot to measure reach, the feel of the roller and how often a tired hand finds the wrong key.
