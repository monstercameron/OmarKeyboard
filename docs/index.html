Omarchy’s shortcuts follow a pattern

Omarchy is an Arch Linux setup built on Hyprland, a tiling window manager that lays windows side by side instead of stacking them. Each workspace is a separate screen of windows, and you drive it all from the keyboard with Super as the desktop modifier.

Super and a digit shows that workspace. Super, Shift and the digit carries the focused window there. Add Alt and the window goes on its own while you stay put. Super and an arrow moves focus in that direction; add Shift and the two windows trade places.

Each action is one chord, and related actions differ by a single modifier. Sending a window away and going with it differ only by Alt, and none of the keys involved say what they do.

Omar-Keyboard gives the actions you repeat most a key each, labeled with the result. Each key sends the stock Omarchy chord, and the chords still work when typed by hand. On this keyboard a workspace is called a desk.

The center of the keyboard is the board you already type on: US QWERTY, a full number row, both Shift keys and a full spacebar. The desktop controls sit around it.

Each group has its own cap color, for finding it by eye, and its own place on the board, for finding it by touch. A raised bar on Menu, Focus and Desk 5 marks the end of each group, like the bars on F and J.

Five desks on the top row

Omarchy gives each monitor ten workspaces. The keyboard labels the first five, Desk 1 to Desk 5, each directly above its digit. Super with a digit still reaches all ten. Last Desk, at the end of the group, returns you to the desk you were on before.

Moving a window takes two taps. Tap Send & Stay, then a desk, and the window goes there while you keep working where you are. Tap Move & Go, then a desk, and you go with it. The keys name the result. With chords alone, the two differ only by Alt.

While a move is waiting, the five desk keys light up for three seconds. Esc or a second tap on the same key cancels it. An arrow or any typing key also cancels it and then does its usual job, so no keystroke is lost. Holding a modifier, turning a dial or rolling the roller leaves the move waiting, so you can hold Shift or lower the volume midway.

Windows under the right hand

Next Window sits beside the right Super key, where the right thumb can reach it. One tap moves to the next window on this desk. Shift goes the other way, and Fn with Next Window returns to the window you used last.

Focus and Swap sit in the bottom-right corner, beside the arrows, and work only while held. Hold Focus and press an arrow to move to the neighboring window, or hold Swap to trade places with it. Because they act only while held, brushing one by accident cannot turn the next arrow into a window command, and letting go hands the arrows straight back to the text cursor.

Above them, Flip Split turns a side-by-side pair into a stacked one, Float lifts a window out of the tiling, and Fullscreen gives it the whole display. Pressing the right dial changes the layout of the entire desk.

What made room

The edges needed space, and it came from three places: the function row, the six-key navigation block and the right-hand Ctrl.

There is no F1 to F12. The top row holds desks and utilities instead, and nothing on the Fn layer brings function keys back. Applications that depend on them need their shortcuts rebound to other keys. This is the largest compromise in the design. It suits Omarchy, where Super shortcuts do the work that function keys do elsewhere.

The navigation block became a scroll roller: a ribbed barrel 42 mm wide above the arrows, broad enough to catch with whichever finger is nearest, with thirty soft detents to a turn. Pull it toward you to read further down a page. Delete moved to the top row, directly above Backspace, so the two directions of deleting share a column.

The right-hand Ctrl became Alt. It sits beside the left arrow, so the right hand can hold it while using the arrows, and it sends a true Right Alt, which Omarchy uses for push-to-talk dictation and which some keyboard layouts need for accented characters.

Caps Lock still toggles when tapped. Held, it becomes Fn, and Fn does only what is printed on the keys in plum: Home, End, PgUp and PgDn on the arrows, Insert on Delete, Last Win on Next Window, split resizing on the right dial and paging on the roller.

Dials and utility keys

The two corner dials are knurled aluminum knobs on Bourns encoders with 24 clicks to a turn. The left one sets the volume and mutes the speakers when pressed. The right one sets screen brightness and, pressed, cycles the desk’s layout. Mic Mute has its own key, so silencing your microphone never touches what you hear.

Lock ends the top row, next to the brightness dial, and is the one key there that interrupts your work. Its cap is about two millimeters lower than its neighbors and its switch, a linear Cherry MX Grey, takes about 80 grams to press where the other keys take 55, so a finger sweeping along the row or reaching for the dial is unlikely to press it by accident.

Screenshot and Grab Text sit together. One captures an image of the screen; the other reads the text in a region and puts it on the clipboard as words you can paste.

Inside the case

The board is built in five layers. From the bottom: the tray, the main circuit board, a 1.5 mm aluminium plate that also carries the scroll roller, 89 tactile Gateron G Pro 3.0 Brown switches with one heavier Cherry MX Grey for Lock, and the keycaps with the two dial knobs. The switches clip into the plate and push into sockets on the circuit board, so none of them is soldered in.

The board to build is Rev B.1. It keeps the Rev A outline, mounting holes and controller bay, which the 3D model on this page still shows, and adds a light under every key. Physical sample testing and the separate USB carrier design are still open.

The main board is one sheet of FR4, 405.81 × 120.06 × 1.6 mm, with its top 5 mm below the plate. It has openings for every switch's pins and a light window under each of the 90 keys. It shares twelve M2 mounting holes with the plate, with two additional supports under the typing span. The tray leaves the Enter stabilizer clear.

To open the case, twelve M2 screws come out from the top, some under a keycap. The plate, switches and board then lift out together as one deck, with the roller still on the plate and the dials still soldered to the board.

The USB-C socket is not on the main board. It sits on a 20 × 7 × 1 mm carrier in a pocket in the rear wall, directly behind Desk 3, together with a fuse, a static-protection chip and the two resistors that let a USB-C host recognise the keyboard. Four wires run from the carrier to the main board, where the regulator, RP2040, 12 MHz crystal and flash occupy the roller bay. The chip clears the tread by 2.00 mm in the assembled model. Two small buttons, BOOT and RUN, are there for loading firmware.

Whether the plug seats fully, how well the carrier stays put when a cable is pulled, and how clean the USB signal is through the short harness can only be measured on a physical sample.

Turned over, the board shows what the switches push into: 90 hot-swap sockets, and 92 diodes, one for each key and each dial press, so the controller can tell any combination of pressed keys apart. The 90 LEDs, one per key, are mounted upside down on this side and shine up through the windows into their keycaps. The roller's encoder runs its three wires down through a slot under the roller's bezel.

The circuit board

Rev B.1 of the main board is drawn and routed in KiCad. This is its copper, drawn from the design files: the top layer in orange, the bottom layer in blue. The controller sits in the bay beside the roller, where its fine-pitch pins have room to spread out on two layers, and four wires bring USB in from the carrier behind Desk 3.

The controller’s pins

The RP2040 seen from above, pin 1 at the dot. 29 of its 30 general-purpose pins are used, so the board needs no second chip to read its 92 contacts and three encoders and to run its 90 lights. Choose a group, or point at a pin, to see where it goes.

A light under every key

Rev B.1 puts an SK6812MINI-E under each of the 90 keys. It shines up through a window in the board and the key’s shine-through legend; equal red, green and blue make white. Fifteen keys also show state in color, at the same brightness as the rest. The dials and the roller are not lit. These are the board’s own controls.

What the board depends on

Fabrication
Two copper layers on 1.6 mm FR-4 with 1 oz copper and an ENIG finish. 0.175 mm traces and clearance, 0.30 mm via drills, and 90 light windows, each 3.50 × 3.05 mm, cut through the board.
Assembly
Both sides populated, including the two through-hole encoders. JLCPCB accepted all 403 placements and showed about three days to make the boards and four to five to assemble them, before shipping.
Power
5 V from USB, with the firmware asking the computer for 500 mA. The lights’ rail stays off until USB is configured and during sleep. Every key white at the 16 of 255 ceiling is estimated at 393 mA for the whole board; the targets to measure are 100 mA before configuration, 450 mA steady and 2.5 mA asleep.
USB
D+ and D− run 11.85 and 11.56 mm on the board with no vias. The signal also passes through the harness and the carrier, so the return path has to be checked across both. The IDs 1209:0001 are private test IDs and need replacing before devices are shared.
USB-C carrier
Not designed yet. A 20 × 7 × 1 mm board in the rear wall with the USB-C socket, a 5.1 kΩ pull-down on each CC pin, low-capacitance static protection and a 500 mA resettable fuse. Its pads must match J1.
Wiring
About 260 to 300 mm of USB-rated twisted pair with power and ground, kept twisted to both ends and laid in three floor saddles. Three wires from the roller’s encoder, with a nominal 3 mm gap between roller and board.
Mounting
Twelve 2.2 mm M2 holes shared with the plate and two supports under the board alone. The board sits 5 mm under the plate, with 24 extra bores for the six stabilized keys.
Switches and caps
89 five-pin Gateron G Pro 3.0 Brown and one Cherry MX Grey, plugged in after assembly. The caps need shine-through legends; opaque dye-sublimated legends would leave only a halo around each key.
Firmware
QMK 0.31.0 with an Arm toolchain. An 8 × 12 matrix scanned column to row with 5 ms debounce, encoders on GP20–GP25, light data on GP26, the light rail on GP27 and the buffer enable on GP28.

Configure the firmware

The QMK keymap the board is designed to ship with. Choose a key to see its keycodes, its place in the switch matrix and what Omarchy receives when it is pressed. Pressing keys runs a copy of the firmware's own key handling, so you can watch a transfer wait for its desk or Caps decide between Caps Lock and Fn. Every keycode and build option you change is written into the firmware files beside it, and a board running VIA can be read and written over USB.
